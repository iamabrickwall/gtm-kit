Most growth teams aren't deciding *whether* to test newsletter sponsorships anymore. They're deciding *which* newsletters to test next, with a real budget and an expectation that the spend will be defensible at the next QBR.

That decision usually breaks the same way: someone forwards a media kit, the subscriber count looks impressive, the open rate looks impressive, the price feels reasonable, and the team commits. Two months later, they don't know whether it worked — but they spent the money.

The problem isn't the testing. It's the signal used to pick what to test.

## Subscriber count is the wrong question

Media kits are seller-reported and unverifiable. A 200,000-subscriber newsletter and a 20,000-subscriber newsletter both look like a single number on a deck, but the number doesn't tell you what you actually need to know: does the audience convert for a brand like yours?

Open rate isn't the answer either. A 45% open rate on an audience that doesn't buy is worse than a 30% open rate on an audience that does. You're not buying eyeballs; you're buying conversion attempts.

So if subscriber count, open rate, and the media kit are all proxies for the question you actually care about, what's the more honest signal?

## Advertisers don't keep paying for placements that don't work

The most reliable signal isn't anything a publisher reports about themselves. It's what other advertisers — especially advertisers like you — keep doing with their budget.

A newsletter where sponsors run for 12 straight months is one where the audience converts. Nobody renews for a year on something that doesn't work. A newsletter where every sponsor runs once and never comes back is telling you something too — even if its subscriber count is enormous.

This is what "follow the repeat money" means in practice. We can't see anyone's private conversion rates. We can see that a competitor renewed their sponsorship for 14 straight months. That fact tells you more than any media kit will.

## A three-question framework: Fit, Proof, Position

The retention lens collapses to three questions. Each one filters out a class of bad bets that the subscriber-count view can't see.

**1. Fit — do companies like me advertise here?**

The type-mix of sponsors reveals who the audience converts for. A newsletter sponsored mostly by devtools companies is telling you something specific if you sell devtools. If you're a B2B SaaS brand looking at a newsletter dominated by consumer fintech sponsors, the audience may be there but it's tuned for someone else's pitch.

Look at sponsor breakdown by vertical, business model, company size, funding stage. Density of *your* category is the fit signal.

**2. Proof — do they keep advertising here?**

This is the decisive question. Long tenure and high repeat rate are the closest thing to a public ROI number a growth team has. A newsletter where 73% of sponsors run again within 60 days is a different bet than one where 12% do.

Filter retention to companies *like you*. "Devtools sponsors here run 9+ months on average" beats a blended average across unrelated verticals. The blended number includes a fitness brand that ran once and quit; that's noise.

**3. Position — where are my competitors, and where's the whitespace?**

Three patterns to read:

- A competitor present *and* long-tenured → validated channel you're absent from. Catch up.
- A competitor present but *churned quickly* → they tested it, it didn't hold. Learn from the exit before you repeat the experiment.
- No one in your category yet *but* strong fit and retention from lookalikes → whitespace. Move first.

## A practical example

A seed-stage devtools company asks: where should we run?

- **TLDR Dev** — 18 devtools sponsors; median tenure 7 months; 6 anchors running 12+ months; two direct competitors active and growing. Strong buy — fit, proof, and competitor validation align.
- **A large general-tech newsletter** — huge list, but devtools sponsors churn in ~6 weeks and repeat rate is 14%. Skip. The audience doesn't convert for this category, regardless of size.
- **A niche infra newsletter** — small subscriber count, but every devtools sponsor that's started is still running 9+ months later, and none of your competitors are there yet. Whitespace bet.

A subscriber-count view would have ranked the general-tech newsletter first. The retention view picks the two that actually work — and flags the trap.

## What this approach won't tell you

Three honest caveats, because the alternative is the kind of breathless category content nobody trusts.

**Retention isn't conversion.** A sponsor running for 12 months may be running because the audience converts, or because they're a strategic brand-builder with patient money. Both are useful signals, but they're different reasons. Treat the data as revealed preference, not direct ROI.

**Coverage is finite for everyone.** No tool — ours included — tracks every newsletter on the internet. The newsletters that matter in any given vertical are the ones to surface. If a publication you care about isn't covered, that's a coverage gap, not a verdict on the publication. Tell us and we'll add it.

**Sample sizes matter.** A newsletter with 3 sponsors and a high repeat rate is interesting but not statistically settled. A newsletter with 40 sponsors and a clear retention pattern is. Treat thin data with appropriate caution.

Used with those caveats, retention is the most honest input to a newsletter buying decision a growth team has. Subscriber count tells you reach. Retention tells you whether it works.
